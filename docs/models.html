Models

The logit family, one floor at a time

The seven ideas say what can go wrong. These are the models researchers use in response. Each one is a small change to the one before, so learn them in order.

First: what is being modelled?

Crash studies ask two kinds of question, and each has its own family of models.

How many?

Crash frequency

The number of crashes on a road segment in a year: 0, 1, 2, 3 … These are counts, modelled with the Poisson model or, when counts vary more than Poisson allows, the negative binomial model.

How bad?

Injury severity

The outcome of one crash: no injury, injury or fatal. These are categories, modelled with the logit family on this page.

Every model below starts the same way. It gives each possible outcome a score: a weighted sum of what is known about the crash, such as speed limit, seat-belt use and lighting. The models differ in how they turn scores into probabilities.

Before the hotel · how many crashes?

Count models: Poisson and negative binomial

When the question is “how many crashes will this segment have in a year?”, the outcome is a count: 0, 1, 2, 3 … The Poisson model is the starting point. It needs one number per segment, the expected count, which is linked to road features (traffic volume, curves, lighting) through escore, so that it can never be negative.

Poisson has a built-in assumption: the variance equals the mean. Real crash counts are more spread out, with many segments at zero and a few with very many. This is called overdispersion, and it is unobserved heterogeneity (idea 1) showing up in counts. The negative binomial model adds one parameter, α, for the extra spread.

Go deeper: zeros, and random parameters for counts

If a data set has even more zeros than the negative binomial expects, zero-inflated models add a separate yes/no part for “is this a segment where crashes essentially never get recorded?”. They are used with care, because a road with traffic is never truly risk-free.

Counts can have random parameters too. A random-parameters negative binomial lets the effect of, say, lane width differ from segment to segment, estimated by simulation exactly as in idea 2.

Model 1 of 6 · Two outcomes: injured or not

Binary logit

The simplest case has two outcomes, such as injured or not injured. The model computes one score and passes it through an S-shaped curve, the logistic function, which turns any number into a probability between 0 and 1.

probability = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score)

A score of 0 gives 50%. Large positive scores approach 100%, large negative scores approach 0%.

Go deeper: odds and the S-curve

The odds of an event are p ÷ (1 − p). A probability of 0.69 is odds of 0.69 ÷ 0.31 = 2.2 to 1. The binary logit says that the logarithm of the odds equals the score, which is where the name comes from: “logit” is short for “log-odds unit”.

Each extra point of score multiplies the odds by e ≈ 2.718. The same change in score moves the probability a lot near 50% and very little near 0% or 100%, which is why the curve is S-shaped.

Model 2 of 6 · Three or more outcomes with no natural order needed

Multinomial logit

With three or more outcomes, each outcome gets its own score. The multinomial logit turns the scores into probabilities in two steps. First, each score becomes a positive weight, escore. Second, each weight is divided by the total of all weights.

The binary logit is the same thing with two outcomes. The multinomial logit is the workhorse of severity analysis and the starting point for everything else on this page.

Its weak spot. The ratio of any two probabilities depends only on those two scores. If one outcome becomes less likely, its lost share is spread over all the others in proportion. This property is called the independence of irrelevant alternatives. It is wrong when some outcomes are close cousins, which is the reason for the next model.

Model 3 of 6 · Some outcomes are more alike than others

Nested logit

“Minor injury” and “severe injury” have something in common that “no injury” lacks: in both, the crash forces reached the occupant. Unrecorded factors that push a crash towards one injury level also push it towards the other.

The nested logit puts such related outcomes in a group, called a nest. One extra number, between 0 and 1, says how alike the nested outcomes are: 1 means not alike at all (the model becomes the plain multinomial logit), and values near 0 mean nearly interchangeable.

Go deeper: the formula in two stages

The nested logit works in two stages. Inside the nest, the outcomes compete like a small multinomial logit, but with their scores divided by the nest setting, which sharpens the competition between them. Between nests, the whole nest competes with the other outcomes using one combined number, called the inclusive value or logsum:

nest weight = (sum of escore ÷ setting over the outcomes in the nest)setting

With two equal scores of 0 and a setting of 0.5 this is (1 + 1)0.5 = 1.414, as in the worked example. The setting is estimated from the data. An estimate near 1 says the nest was not needed; an estimate outside the range 0 to 1 is a sign that the nesting structure is wrong.

Model 4 of 6 · Outcomes have a natural order

Ordered logit and probit

No injury, injury and fatal have a natural order. The ordered logit (and its close relative the ordered probit) uses that order. It computes a single score for the crash, a hidden “severity tendency”, and cuts the scale with thresholds. Where the tendency falls decides the level.

It needs fewer parameters than the multinomial logit, because there is one score, not one per outcome. The price is a restriction: a variable that raises the score must make the highest level more likely and the lowest level less likely. Some real effects do not behave that way. An airbag can reduce fatalities and also increase minor injuries.

The ordered probit is the same idea with a normal curve in place of the logistic curve. Results are usually very similar.

Go deeper: thresholds, and when the ordered model is too strict

The thresholds are not chosen by the analyst. They are estimated together with the coefficients. With three levels there are two thresholds; software fixes one number (the first threshold or the constant) so that the rest can be pinned down.

Every variable has one coefficient, which shifts the whole curve. That is the “parallel” assumption: a variable cannot make the middle level more likely and both ends less likely. Extensions relax this. The generalised ordered model lets the thresholds themselves depend on variables, and random-parameters ordered models add the heterogeneity of idea 1.

The probit version replaces the logistic curve with a normal curve. In the example, no injury would be Φ(0 − 0.5) = 0.309 instead of 0.378.

Model 5 of 6 · Effects differ from person to person

Mixed (random parameters) logit

All models so far give every person the same effects. The mixed logit, also called the random parameters logit, removes that assumption. It is the multinomial logit with idea 1 built in: each person has their own effect, drawn from a mixing distribution.

The probability it reports is the average of the logit probability over all those personal effects. That average has no exact formula, so it is computed by simulation, which is idea 2.

Two extensions are common in recent papers. Heterogeneity in means and variances lets the centre and the spread of a random effect depend on other variables, for example a larger seat-belt benefit for younger occupants. Correlated random parameters let two random effects move together.

Go deeper: how one coefficient becomes a distribution

In a multinomial logit the “no seat belt” coefficient for fatal might be +1.6 for everyone. In a mixed logit it is written as

coefficient for person i = mean + spread × (a random draw for person i)

With mean 1.6 and spread 1.0, a person whose draw is −2 has a coefficient of −0.4 and a person whose draw is +1 has 2.6. The model estimates the mean and the spread. If the spread is not statistically different from zero, the coefficient is kept fixed.

Heterogeneity in means lets the mean depend on something observed: mean = 1.6 + 0.5 × (occupant older than 65). Heterogeneity in variances does the same for the spread. Both help explain why effects differ, not only that they do.

The probability for a crash is the multinomial-logit probability averaged over the draws, computed by simulation with, typically, several hundred to a thousand or more Halton draws.

Model 6 of 6 · A few hidden groups, each with its own effects

Latent class logit

The mixed logit lets effects vary smoothly. The latent class logit takes a different view: crashes belong to a small number of hidden groups, and each group has its own multinomial logit. “Latent” means hidden. Nobody labels the groups; the model infers them.

The model estimates the share of each class and the effects within each class. It does not need the analyst to choose a mixing distribution, which is its main appeal. Its limit is that everyone inside a class is treated as identical. Some papers combine the two and allow random parameters inside each class.

Go deeper: who is in which class?

Class membership is itself a small multinomial logit. Each class has a score, possibly depending on crash characteristics, and the shares are escore ÷ total, exactly as on floor 1.

After estimation, each crash gets a probability of belonging to each class. Using the example (class A: 60%, severe 0.10; class B: 40%, severe 0.40), a crash that was severe belongs to class B with probability 0.16 ÷ 0.22 = 0.73.

The number of classes is chosen by fitting models with two, three, four classes and comparing them with information criteria such as BIC, which reward fit and penalise extra parameters.

Remember it · Floor 5 · the masked ball. Two secret clubs are at a masked ball. You cannot tell who belongs to which club. You only know that 60% wear one kind of ring under their glove, and each club dances to its own rules.

Which model when?

ModelUse it whenWatch out for
Poisson, negative binomialThe outcome is a count of crashesPoisson assumes mean = variance; crash counts usually vary more
Binary logitTwo outcomesNothing beyond the issues below
Multinomial logitThree or more outcomes; a flexible first modelTreats all outcomes as equally unrelated
Nested logitSome outcomes share unrecorded causesYou must decide the nests
Ordered logit or probitOutcomes are ordered and you want few parametersOne score moves all levels the same way
Mixed logitEffects differ across people (idea 1)Needs simulation and a choice of mixing distribution
Latent class logitYou suspect a few distinct hidden groupsEveryone inside a class is identical; the number of classes is a choice
