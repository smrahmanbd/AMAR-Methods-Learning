Idea 1 of 7

Unobserved heterogeneity

Suppose a model says that wearing a seat belt lowers injury severity by a certain amount. A standard model gives one number for everybody. In reality the benefit depends on things the crash report never records: how the person was sitting, their bone strength, the exact angle of impact. This unrecorded variation is called unobserved heterogeneity.

A random-parameters model lets the effect differ from person to person. The analyst chooses a shape for how the effects are spread out. That shape is called the mixing distribution. The model then estimates its centre and its spread.

Other ways to capture heterogeneity include letting the mean or the variance of the random parameter depend on other variables, and latent-class models, which sort observations into a few hidden groups. Mannering, Shankar and Bhat (2016) review them and note that no single approach is clearly best.

Remember it · stop 1, the costume shop. Every customer who walks through the same door comes out in a different costume. One door (one variable), many outcomes (many effects). The shopkeeper sorts the costumes onto a bell-shaped rail: the mixing distribution.

Idea 2 of 7

Simulated maximum likelihood

A random-parameters model has a problem: to compute a probability it must average over every possible value of the effect. That average is an integral with no exact formula.

The solution is simulation. Draw a value of the effect, compute the probability for that value, repeat many times, and take the average. The more draws, the closer the average gets to the true answer. Estimating a model this way is called simulated maximum likelihood.

Halton draws are a trick to need fewer draws. Random draws clump by chance. Halton draws follow a fixed pattern that covers the range evenly, so the average settles sooner.

Idea 3 of 7

Endogeneity

Road agencies put ice-warning signs where ice crashes have happened. Now compare segments with signs to segments without. The signed segments have more crashes. Do signs cause crashes?

No. The ice causes both the sign and the crashes. The sign is endogenous: it is a response to the very risk we are trying to measure. Mannering and Bhat (2014) use this example. The same trap appears with personal choices. If safer drivers are the ones who buy cars with side airbags, the airbag looks better than it is.

Cures include instrumental variables, control functions and joint models of the choice and the outcome. All of them try to separate the part of the variable that is a response to risk from the part that is not.

Idea 4 of 7

Spatial and temporal correlation

Two neighbouring road segments share weather, drainage, driver population and enforcement. If the model does not include these, what it fails to explain on one segment will look like what it fails to explain next door. The same happens over time: this year’s unexplained crashes at a site resemble last year’s.

A model that treats every observation as independent then believes it has more information than it does. Its estimates look more certain than they are, and they can also be biased. Spatial and temporal models add an explicit link between neighbours and between years.

Idea 5 of 7

Temporal instability

Cars get safer, phones enter the cabin, a recession changes who drives, a pandemic empties the roads. The way a variable affects crashes can shift with each of these. Mannering (2018) calls this temporal instability, and a large body of work has since found that parameters estimated in one period often differ in the next.

The standard check: estimate one model on all years together, then a separate model for each period. If the separate models fit much better, the effects are not stable. A likelihood ratio test says how much better counts as “much”.

Idea 6 of 7

Prediction versus causality

Two different questions hide in most safety studies. Prediction: how many crashes will this site have next year? Causality: if I change this one thing, how will crashes change? A model can be excellent at the first and wrong about the second.

Mannering, Bhat, Shankar and Abdel-Aty (2020) give an example of why. In bad weather, cautious drivers stay home. The drivers observed in snow are a self-selected, riskier group. The data then mix the effect of snow with the effect of who is driving.

Data-driven methods such as machine learning tend to predict well but say little about cause. Statistical models built to handle heterogeneity and endogeneity predict less sharply but support causal statements. The choice depends on the question.

Idea 7 of 7

Crash–conflict relationship

Crashes are rare. Waiting years to count them is slow, and each one is a tragedy. Traffic conflicts, or near misses, happen far more often and can be measured from video or vehicle sensors within days.

The most used measures are time-to-collision (how many seconds until two road users would collide if neither changed course) and post-encroachment time (Arun, Haque, Sayed and Mannering, 2021). The smaller the number, the closer the call. A crash is the extreme case: time-to-collision of zero.

Extreme value theory is the statistics of rare extremes. It fits a curve to the most severe near misses and extends that curve to zero, giving an estimate of crash probability from near misses alone (Ali, Haque and Mannering, 2023).
