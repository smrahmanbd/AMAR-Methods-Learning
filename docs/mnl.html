The multinomial logit from zero

This page assumes nothing. It follows one crash through the model, from what the police report says to three probabilities, and then explains where the model’s numbers come from and how to read them.

Step 1 of 8

What question does it answer?

A crash has happened. It ended in one of several ways: no injury, injury or fatal. We know a few facts about it, such as whether the driver wore a seat belt. The multinomial logit answers one question:

Given what we know about this crash, how likely was each outcome?

“Multinomial” means “more than two named outcomes”. “Logit” is the name of the formula it uses. The model does not say what will happen. It gives a probability for each outcome, and the probabilities add up to 100%.

Step 2 of 8

Give every outcome a score

The model keeps a small table of numbers called coefficients. Each outcome has its own row. To score a crash, start with the outcome’s constant and add the coefficient of every fact that is true for this crash. Papers call the score the utility or the severity function.

No injury (base)0000

Fatal−2.5+1.6+1.1+0.6

Step 3 of 8

Turn scores into weights, then into shares

Scores can be negative, and probabilities cannot. So the model first turns each score into a positive weight with the exponential function, escore (e ≈ 2.718). A score of 0 gives a weight of 1. Each extra point of score multiplies the weight by 2.718.

Then each outcome’s probability is simply its share of the total weight. That is the whole model:

probability of an outcome = eits score ÷ (sum of escore over all outcomes)

Step 4 of 8

Why one outcome has a score of zero

Add 5 to every score and nothing changes: every weight is multiplied by the same amount, so the shares stay the same. Only differences between scores matter.

That means the model has one score too many. The fix is to pick one outcome, call it the base outcome, and set its score to 0. Every coefficient is then read relative to the base. In the table above the base is “no injury”, so +1.6 means “not wearing a seat belt pushes a crash towards fatal, compared with no injury”.

Choosing a different base changes the numbers in the table but not a single probability.

Step 5 of 8

How to read the numbers in a paper

1. The sign. A positive coefficient pushes crashes towards that outcome, relative to the base. A negative one pulls them away.

2. The odds ratio. “Odds” here means the probability of an outcome divided by the probability of the base outcome. The exponential of a coefficient tells you by how much the odds are multiplied. For fatal, e1.6 ≈ 4.95: without a seat belt, the odds of a fatal outcome against no injury are about five times higher.

3. The change in probability. This is what most people want, and papers report it as a marginal effect. It is the difference in probability with and without the fact, holding everything else the same. Unlike the coefficient, it depends on the rest of the crash.

Step 6 of 8

Where do the coefficients come from?

Nobody chooses the coefficients. The computer finds them from data by maximum likelihood: it tries values, and for each try it asks, “if these values were true, how probable is the data we actually observed?” It keeps the values that make the observed data most probable.

Probabilities of thousands of crashes multiplied together give a tiny number, so the computer works with its logarithm, the log-likelihood. It is always negative, and closer to zero is better.

Step 7 of 8

How good is the fit?

Compare the log-likelihood of your model with that of a model that knows nothing. A common summary is McFadden’s pseudo R-squared:

ρ² = 1 − (log-likelihood of the model) ÷ (log-likelihood of the know-nothing model)

It runs from 0 (no better than knowing nothing) towards 1. It is not the share of variance explained, and values that would look low for an ordinary regression are normal here.

Step 8 of 8

The weak spot

The model treats outcomes as unrelated. Its formula implies that the ratio of any two probabilities depends only on those two scores. This is called the independence of irrelevant alternatives.

Words you will meet in papers

WordPlain meaning
Alternative, outcomeOne of the possible results: no injury, injury, fatal.
Utility, severity functionThe score of an outcome for one crash.
Coefficient, parameter (β)A number in the table: how much one fact adds to a score.
ConstantThe score an outcome gets before any fact is added.
Base outcomeThe outcome whose score is fixed at 0; everything is read relative to it.
LikelihoodHow probable the observed data are, given the coefficients.
Log-likelihoodThe logarithm of the likelihood. Negative; closer to zero is better.
Odds ratioe to the power of a coefficient: how much a fact multiplies the odds against the base.
Marginal effectThe change in an outcome’s probability when a fact changes.
Independence of irrelevant alternativesThe weak spot: lost probability is shared out in proportion among all other outcomes.

A pump inflates each dish’s balloon to e to the power of its score (the weight). Your chance of choosing a dish is its balloon’s share of all the air. One dish, plain bread, always has a balloon of size 1 (the base outcome).
