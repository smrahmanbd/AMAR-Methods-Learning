From logit to RPLHMV

How crash-severity models grow, one idea at a time: likelihood, the multinomial logit, random parameters, and heterogeneity in means and variances.

What the four names mean

Short nameFull nameIn one line
MNLMultinomial logitOne fixed effect per variable, the same for every crash.
RPLRandom parameters logit (also called mixed logit)The effect varies from crash to crash, following a bell curve.
RPLHMRPL with heterogeneity in the meansThe centre of that bell moves with things we can observe (for example the driver's age).
RPLHMVRPL with heterogeneity in the means and variancesThe centre moves and the width of the bell changes with observed things (for example night-time).

Memory hook: fixed → spread → shift → stretch. Picture one coefficient as a jelly. MNL is a toothpick (one value). RPL is a wobbly jelly (a spread of values). RPLHM slides the jelly along the table. RPLHMV also stretches or squeezes it.

One running example, all lecture long

A driver is in a crash. What happened to them?

  • Outcome 0: No injury (our base)
  • Outcome 1: Minor injury
  • Outcome 2: Severe injury

One explanatory variable: Unbelted = 1 if the driver wore no seat belt, 0 if they did.

Later we add two more: Z = young driver, W = night-time crash.

All numbers are invented for teaching, but the steps are exactly those of a real study.

Probability, odds and log-odds

Three ways to say the same thing.

Probability pOdds = p ÷ (1 − p)Log-odds = ln(odds)Read as
0.5010as likely as not
0.7531.103 to 1 for
0.200.25−1.394 to 1 against
0.010.0101−4.60almost never

Why bother? Probability is stuck between 0 and 1. Log-odds can be any number from −∞ to +∞, so we can safely model it as a straight line: log-odds = β₀ + β₁x.

Ask: what are the odds if p = 0.9? (9.) And the log-odds? (2.20.) Negative log-odds always means p below one half.

The binary logit: a score becomes a probability

P(severe) = ez ÷ (1 + ez) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z),   where z = β₀ + β₁xz is the score (the log-odds). β₀ is the intercept, β₁ the effect of x.
Default sliders: β₀ = −1, β₁ = 0.9, x = 2 give z = 0.8 and P = 0.690. Work it on the board: e^0.8 = 2.226; 2.226 ÷ 3.226 = 0.690.

Reading a coefficient

  • Sign: β > 0 raises the probability; β < 0 lowers it.
  • Size: β is a change in log-odds per unit of x. Not directly a change in probability.
  • Odds ratio: eβ multiplies the odds. β = 0.9 → e0.9 = 2.46: each unit of x makes the odds 2.46 times larger.
  • t-statistic = β ÷ standard error. Roughly, |t| > 1.96 means the effect is distinguishable from zero at the 5% level.

A coefficient of 0.9 does not mean "90% more likely". To talk in probabilities, we compute marginal effects (coming later).

What is likelihood?

"If my guess were true, how probable is exactly the data I saw?"

  • We saw 10 crashes; 3 were severe.
  • Guess p = chance a crash is severe. The probability of this exact pattern is p × p × p × (1 − p) × … seven times.
  • So the likelihood is L(p) = p3(1 − p)7.
  • Probability: parameters fixed, data vary. Likelihood: data fixed, parameters vary.
The order of the crashes does not matter for finding the best p (a constant multiplier never moves the peak), so we leave out the binomial coefficient.

Try guesses: which p explains the data best?

Move p slowly from 0.1 to 0.9. Then press the button. The peak sits at 0.3 = 3/10, the share we observed. That is the maximum likelihood estimate.

By hand: likelihood at two guesses

Guess p = 0.5

  1. L = 0.53 × 0.57 = 0.510
  2. = 0.000977
  3. LL = 10 × ln 0.5 = 10 × (−0.6931)
  4. = −6.931

Guess p = 0.3

  1. L = 0.33 × 0.77 = 0.027 × 0.08235
  2. = 0.002224
  3. LL = 3 × ln 0.3 + 7 × ln 0.7 = 3 × (−1.204) + 7 × (−0.357)
  4. = −3.612 − 2.497 = −6.109

Rule: higher likelihood = better explanation. Log-likelihood is always ≤ 0 for discrete outcomes; closer to zero is better. −6.109 beats −6.931.

Why the log-likelihood?

L = P1 × P2 × … × PN   ⟶   LL = ln P1 + ln P2 + … + ln PNThe log of a product is the sum of the logs. The log never changes where the peak is.
  • Sums are easy to differentiate and add up across thousands of crashes.
  • Products of thousands of small numbers underflow to 0 on a computer.

The log-likelihood of a crash model

LL(β) = Σn=1..N Σi δin × ln Pn(i)δin = 1 if crash n had outcome i, else 0. So each crash contributes the log of the probability the model gave to what really happened.
  • Crash 1 was severe; model said P(severe) = 0.32 → contributes ln 0.32 = −1.14.
  • Crash 2 was no injury; model said P(no injury) = 0.55 → contributes ln 0.55 = −0.60.
  • Add up over all crashes. A model that gives high probabilities to what happened gets an LL near zero.

Maximum likelihood estimation: climbing the hill

Key points: the estimate is the top of the hill; standard errors come from how curved the top is (sharp top → small standard error). Software uses smarter steps (Newton-type methods such as BFGS), but the picture is the same.

Three reference log-likelihoods

  • LL(0): every coefficient zero, so every outcome equally likely. With 3 outcomes and N crashes, LL(0) = N × ln(1/3). For N = 2,500: −2,746.5.
  • LL(c): constants only. Reproduces the overall shares, nothing else.
  • LL(β): the full model at convergence.
ρ² = 1 − LL(β) ÷ LL(0)McFadden's pseudo R². For an invented MNL with LL(β) = −2,450.3: ρ² = 1 − 2,450.3 ÷ 2,746.5 = 0.108. Values of 0.1 to 0.3 are common; it is not the R² of regression.

AIC and BIC: fit minus a penalty for size

AIC = 2K − 2LLK = number of estimated parameters.
BIC = K ln N − 2LLHeavier penalty when N is large.
  • Smaller is better for both.
  • They can compare models that are not nested (where the likelihood ratio test cannot be used).
  • Invented MNL: K = 12, LL = −2,450.3 → AIC = 24 + 4,900.6 = 4,924.6; BIC = 12 × ln 2,500 + 4,900.6 = 4,994.5.

The likelihood ratio test

LR = −2 × [LL(restricted) − LL(unrestricted)]  ~  χ² with df = number of restrictionsRestricted = the simpler model, obtained by locking some parameters (often to zero). Unrestricted = the richer model.
Start with the coin preset: LR = 2 × (−6.109 + 6.931) = 1.65, below 3.84, so a fair coin is not rejected. Then the ladder presets.

More than two outcomes: one score each

Sin = βiXin + εinS = severity "propensity" of outcome i for crash n; βX = the part we can explain; ε = the part we cannot see.
  • Our example: Vno = 0 (base),   Vminor = −0.5 + 0.8 × Unbelted,   Vsevere = −1.5 + 1.6 × Unbelted.
  • Why a base? Only differences between scores matter. Adding the same number to every score changes nothing, so one outcome is pinned at 0.
  • Each outcome can have its own variables. In crash studies, a variable is usually placed in one or two outcomes' functions only.

The multinomial logit formula

Pn(i) = eβiXin ÷ ΣI eβIXInEach outcome's weight divided by the total weight. Also called the softmax.

By hand: belted versus unbelted

Belted (Unbelted = 0)

  1. Scores: 0, −0.5, −1.5
  2. Weights: 1, 0.6065, 0.2231; total 1.8297
  3. Shares: 0.547, 0.331, 0.122

Unbelted (Unbelted = 1)

  1. Scores: 0, 0.3, 0.1
  2. Weights: 1, 1.3499, 1.1052; total 3.4550
  3. Shares: 0.289, 0.391, 0.320

Result: not wearing a belt raises the probability of severe injury from 12.2% to 32.0%, a change of +0.198.

Why this formula? Gumbel errors

McFadden (1973) showed that if the ε's are independent and identically distributed type I extreme value (Gumbel), the probability that outcome i has the largest S is exactly the MNL formula.

Two weak spots of the multinomial logit

1. IIA

Independence of irrelevant alternatives: the ratio of any two probabilities ignores every other outcome. Splitting Severe into two identical copies wrongly increases total severe share (try the split button on the formula slide).

2. One effect for everyone

βunbelted = 1.6 for every crash. But unbelted drivers differ in ways the data do not record: vehicle size, body strength, how they sit, airbag deployment. The real effect differs crash by crash: unobserved heterogeneity.

Ignoring heterogeneity can give biased, inconsistent estimates and wrong conclusions (Mannering, Shankar and Bhat, 2016). The next rung fixes weak spot 2, and eases weak spot 1 too.

Let the effect vary: random parameters

βn = β + σ × νn,   νn ~ Normal(0, 1)β = the average effect, σ = how much it varies across crashes, νn = this crash's unseen draw.
  • Each crash has its own coefficient, drawn from a distribution (usually normal; lognormal, triangular and uniform are also used).
  • We do not learn each crash's β. We estimate the mean β and the spread σ.
  • If σ is not significantly different from zero, the parameter is fixed after all.

See the spread

By hand: share of crashes where being unbelted lowers severity = P(βn < 0) = Φ(−1.6 ÷ 1.2) = Φ(−1.333) = 0.091. About 9% protective, 91% harmful.

Papers report exactly this: "the parameter is normally distributed with mean 1.6 and standard deviation 1.2, so it is greater than zero for 90.9% of observations". A negative share for a seat belt is possible in a model; ask the audience to think of reasons (data errors, ejection versus entrapment, unrecorded factors).

The probability becomes an integral

Pn(i) = ∫ [ eβiXin ÷ ΣI eβIXIn ] f(β | φ) dβThe MNL probability, averaged over the bell f(β | φ), where φ = (mean, spread). No closed form.
  • Solution: simulate. Draw R values of β, compute the MNL probability for each, average.
  • The log of these simulated probabilities, summed over crashes, is the simulated log-likelihood. Maximising it is simulated maximum likelihood.

Practical points about random parameters

  • Halton draws cover the bell evenly and need far fewer draws than random ones. Crash studies often use several hundred to 1,000 Halton draws.
  • Which parameters are random? Try each candidate; keep it random if its σ is statistically significant.
  • Distribution: normal is the usual start. Lognormal forces one sign. Compare by log-likelihood.
  • Correlation: two random parameters can be allowed to move together (correlated random parameters).
  • Results can change with the draws: check that estimates are stable when you increase R.

Change in the mean: the bell slides

βn = β + Θ × Zn + σ × νnZn = an observed variable for crash n (here: young driver). Θ = how much the mean shifts when Z changes by one.
  • RPL says: effects vary, for unknown reasons.
  • RPLHM adds: part of that variation is linked to something we observe.
  • Example: Θ = 0.5. For young drivers the mean effect of being unbelted is 1.6 + 0.5 = 2.1; for others it stays 1.6.
  • The spread σ is unchanged: the bell keeps its shape and simply moves.

Slide the bell

By hand (young drivers): mean = 2.1, σ = 1.2. Share with a protective effect = Φ(−2.1 ÷ 1.2) = Φ(−1.75) = 0.040, down from 0.091. Average P(severe | unbelted) rises from 35.7% to 45.1%.

Press the Young driver button on and off: the dots move together. That is what "heterogeneity in the mean" looks like.

Reading Θ

  • Θ > 0: the variable Z makes the random parameter larger on average.
  • Θ < 0: Z makes it smaller on average.
  • A t-test on Θ tells whether the mean really depends on Z.
  • Z can be anything observed: driver characteristics, road, weather, time, vehicle.
  • Restriction to test: Θ = 0 turns RPLHM back into RPL.

Jelly picture: the young-driver jelly sits further to the right on the table. Same shape, new place.

Change in the variance: the bell stretches

βn = β + Θ × Zn + σ × eω × Wn × νnWn = an observed variable (here: night). ω = how much the spread stretches (ω > 0) or shrinks (ω < 0).
  • Why the exponential? It keeps the spread positive whatever ω and W are.
  • Example: ω = −0.4. At night the spread is 1.2 × e−0.4 = 1.2 × 0.670 = 0.804.
  • Meaning: at night, crashes are more alike in how much being unbelted matters.
This is the form used by Seraneeprakarn et al. (2017) and many later papers in Analytic Methods in Accident Research.

Shift and stretch together

By hand (night, not young): mean 1.6, spread 0.804. Protective share = Φ(−1.6 ÷ 0.804) = Φ(−1.99) = 0.023. A narrower bell puts less of itself below zero.

Marginal effects: talk in probabilities

  • For an indicator (0/1) variable: P(x = 1) − P(x = 0), computed for each crash, then averaged.
  • With random parameters, each crash's probabilities are themselves averages over draws.
Hou et al. (2022) discuss how to compute marginal effects and out-of-sample predictions properly with random parameters models.

A study, step by step

  1. Code the data: outcomes and 0/1 indicators.
  2. Estimate an MNL; keep variables with |t| > 1.96 (or another stated rule).
  3. Try each parameter as random (normal; 500–1,000 Halton draws). Keep those with significant σ.
  4. Try observed variables Z in the means of the random parameters (Θ).
  5. Try observed variables W in the variances (ω).
  6. Compare with likelihood ratio tests, AIC and BIC.
  7. Report coefficients, σ, Θ, ω, the share above/below zero and average marginal effects.
  8. Check stability: more draws, different starting values, temporal and spatial transferability tests.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a coefficient as a change in probability. Use marginal effects.
  • Comparing LL of models estimated on different data. LL only compares models on the same crashes.
  • Using the LR test for non-nested models. Use AIC/BIC instead.
  • Using too few draws, or not checking that results are stable as draws increase.
  • Predicting with only the mean of a random parameter. The spread changes predicted probabilities (0.320 versus 0.357).
  • Forgetting that a significant Θ or ω is a statistical association, not proof of cause.
