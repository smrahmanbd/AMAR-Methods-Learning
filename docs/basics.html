Logit and likelihood from zero

Two ideas carry almost every paper in this field: the logit, which turns a score into a probability, and the likelihood ratio test, which decides whether a more complicated model is worth it. This page builds both from school mathematics.

Building block 1 of 8

Probability, odds and log-odds

The same chance can be written three ways.

  • Probability: 20 injuries in 100 crashes is a probability of 0.20. It lives between 0 and 1.
  • Odds: injuries against non-injuries, 20 to 80, or 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 0.25. Odds live between 0 and infinity.
  • Log-odds: the natural logarithm of the odds, ln(0.25) = −1.39. Log-odds can be any number, negative or positive.

A model adds things up, and a sum can be any number. Only the log-odds scale can hold any number, so that is the scale the model works on. The log-odds is the score.

Building block 2 of 8

Two buttons on the calculator: e and ln

ex (the exponential) turns any number into a positive one. ln (the natural logarithm) undoes it. They are opposites, like squaring and taking a square root.

Three facts are enough for this whole site. e0 = 1. Adding in the exponent means multiplying outside: ea + b = ea × eb. And the logarithm of a product is a sum: ln(a × b) = ln(a) + ln(b).

Building block 3 of 8

The logit in one line

Put blocks 1 and 2 together. The model says the log-odds of an outcome equals a score:

ln( p ÷ (1 − p) ) = score = constant + coefficient × indicator + …

Solve for p and you get the S-shaped logistic formula, p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score). “Logit” simply means log-odds.

What a coefficient means follows directly: one unit of a variable adds the coefficient to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by ecoefficient. That multiplier is the odds ratio. With more than two outcomes, each outcome has its own score and the odds are taken against the base outcome.

Building block 4 of 8

Likelihood: how probable is what we saw?

A model with given coefficients assigns a probability to every possible outcome. The likelihood is the probability it assigned to the outcomes that actually happened, all multiplied together.

Building block 5 of 8

Log-likelihood: the same thing on a friendlier scale

Multiply thousands of probabilities and the result is too small for a computer to hold. Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum.

  • It is the sum of ln(probability of what happened) over all crashes.
  • Every probability is below 1, so every logarithm is negative. A log-likelihood is always negative.
  • Closer to zero is better. A value of −588 beats −612.
  • It grows with the sample, so a log-likelihood means nothing alone. Only compare models fitted to the same data.

Building block 6 of 8

The likelihood ratio test

Adding parameters can never make the fit worse. So the question is never “does the bigger model fit better?” (it always does) but “does it fit better by more than chance alone would give?”

The test compares two models fitted to the same data:

  • the restricted model, which is simpler: some parameters are forced to be zero or equal;
  • the unrestricted model, which is free.
Restricted and unrestricted models, animated

statistic = −2 × [ LL(restricted) − LL(unrestricted) ]

The statistic is zero if the two fit equally well and grows as the restricted model falls behind. If the restriction is really true, the statistic follows a known pattern, the chi-square distribution. Its shape depends on the degrees of freedom: the number of parameters the restriction removed. The critical value is the point that chance alone would exceed only 5% of the time.

Why the −2? It is the scaling that makes the statistic follow the chi-square distribution. Why “ratio”? A difference of logarithms is the logarithm of a ratio: the test compares the two likelihoods by dividing one by the other.

Building block 7 of 8

Reading the verdict carefully

“Rejected”

The simpler model fits significantly worse. Keep the larger model: the extra parameters, the separate road classes or the separate years are needed.

“Not rejected”

The simpler model is good enough for these data. It does not prove the restriction is true, only that the data cannot tell the two apart.

Confidence level

At 95%, a true restriction is wrongly rejected 5 times in 100. Papers often report 90%, 95% and 99%. Run forty tests and a couple of false alarms are expected.

Size is not importance

With tens of thousands of crashes, tiny differences become “significant”. Always look at the marginal effects to see whether a difference matters in practice.

The p-value is the chance of a statistic at least this large if the restriction were true. A p-value of 0.03 passes at 95% and fails at 99%.

Two rules the test needs. The models must be fitted to the same crashes, and one must be a restricted version of the other (they must be nested). For models that are not nested, use the yardsticks below.

Remember it · the courtroom of Judge Chi. Two tailors bring suits for the same customer. One suit is plain (the restricted model); the other has extra pockets (the unrestricted model). Judge Chi measures how much better the fancy suit fits, doubles it, and holds it against a ruler. The ruler has one notch for every extra pocket (degrees of freedom). Past the red notch, the pockets are worth it. Short of it, the plain suit wins.
