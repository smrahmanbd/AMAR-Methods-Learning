Case study

A full severity study, step by step

Many recent papers follow the same recipe: split crashes by place and by year, fit a random-parameters logit to each part, then test whether the parts can share a model. This page walks through that recipe with a study of truck crashes on three classes of road over three years. Every number is invented for teaching.

The study in one picture

The question: do the factors linked to crash severity differ between expressways, dual carriageways and single carriageways, and do they stay the same from year to year?

The outcome has three levels: property damage only, injury, fatal. The design is 3 road classes × 3 years = 9 separate models, compared with each other by likelihood ratio tests.

Step 1 of 10

Turn the crash records into 0/1 indicators

A model needs numbers. Each category in the records becomes an indicator: 1 if true for this crash, 0 if not. One category of each variable gets no indicator. It is the base category, and every coefficient is read as “compared with the base”.

Step 2 of 10

Split the data into subsets

To ask whether effects differ by place and year, the data are split first. Three road classes and three years give nine subsets, each with its own model. This is called segmentation.

Before modelling, a quick chi-square test checks whether the mix of severities differs between groups at all. It compares the counts you observed with the counts you would expect if road class made no difference.

The trade-off in splitting: more subsets answer finer questions, but each has fewer crashes. A subset with very few fatal crashes gives shaky estimates, which is one reason to test whether two road classes can be merged (step 8).

Step 3 of 10

Start with the multinomial logit

Each subset first gets a plain multinomial logit. Property damage only is the base outcome with a score of 0. Injury and fatal each get a constant and their own coefficients.

score of outcome i for crash n = constant + (coefficients × indicators) + a random disturbance

The disturbance stands for everything not in the data. Assuming it follows an extreme value distribution is what produces the simple “weight ÷ total of weights” formula. Because the outcomes are treated as unordered, a variable can raise the fatal share and lower the injury share at once, which an ordered model cannot do.

A variable can enter at most two of the three score functions. Entering all three would be like adding the same number to every score: nothing would change.

The multinomial logit from zero

Step 4 of 10

Choose which variables stay

With thirty or so candidate indicators, not all will matter. The usual tool is the t-statistic: the estimate divided by its standard error. It says how many standard errors the estimate is away from zero. A common rule keeps a variable when |t| ≥ 1.645, which corresponds to 90% confidence.

Backward selection starts with everything in, removes the weakest variable, estimates again, and repeats until every remaining variable passes.

Step 5 of 10

Let effects vary: random parameters with heterogeneity in means and variances

Now each coefficient is tested as a random parameter: one that differs from crash to crash (idea 1). This model is often written RPLHMV: random parameters logit with heterogeneity in means and variances. The coefficient for crash n is

βn = β + Θ × Zn + σ × eω × Wn × (a standard normal draw)

  • β is the mean effect and σ its spread. With only these two, it is an ordinary random parameter.
  • Θ × Z is heterogeneity in the mean: for crashes where indicator Z is 1, the whole curve slides by Θ.
  • eω × W is heterogeneity in the variance: for crashes where indicator W is 1, the spread is multiplied by eω.

Z and W are other variables in the data. They help explain why the effect differs, not just that it does.

When is a parameter kept as random? Two checks: its spread σ must be statistically significant, and a likelihood ratio test must favour the random-parameter model over the fixed one (step 8’s calculator has a “Random vs fixed” mode). The random draws are usually assumed normal.

Step 6 of 10

Estimate by simulation

The probability of an outcome is now the multinomial-logit probability averaged over all possible draws of the random parameters. That average is an integral with no exact solution, so it is simulated (idea 2).

A practical routine: use a modest number of Halton draws, for example 200, while searching for the best specification, because hundreds of trial models must be estimated. Then re-estimate the final models with many more, for example 1,000, so that the reported numbers do not depend on the draws.

Step 7 of 10

Report average marginal effects

Coefficients of a logit model are hard to interpret directly. The marginal effect answers a plainer question: by how many percentage points does the probability of an outcome change when an indicator goes from 0 to 1?

The answer differs from crash to crash. So it is computed for every crash and averaged:

average marginal effect = average over all crashes of [ P(outcome | indicator = 1) − P(outcome | indicator = 0) ]

Step 8 of 10

Test whether models transfer between road classes

Do expressways and dual carriageways need different models? The likelihood ratio test answers with one comparison. Take the estimated parameters of road class A, apply them unchanged to the crashes of road class B, and see how much worse they fit than B’s own model:

χ² = −2 × [ LL(A’s parameters on B’s data) − LL(B’s own model) ]

A large value means A’s parameters do not describe B. The test is run in both directions (A on B, and B on A) for every pair of road classes in every year.

A second version asks whether two classes can be merged: fit one pooled model and compare it with the two separate ones.

χ² = −2 × [ LL(pooled) − LL(class 1) − LL(class 2) ]

Step 9 of 10

Test stability over time, then find what is stable

The same test is used across years (idea 5): apply the 2021 parameters to the 2020 crashes and compare with the 2020 model, in both directions, for every pair of years and every road class.

If the years differ, a natural next question is: which effects changed and which stayed the same? A partially temporally constrained model pools the years of one road class and forces a parameter to be equal across years whenever a test does not reject equality. The others stay free. The constrained model is then compared with the fully separate yearly models:

χ² = −2 × [ LL(constrained) − sum of LL(each year) ], degrees of freedom = parameters saved

If this test is not rejected, the constraints are acceptable, and the constrained parameters are the temporally stable effects: the ones most useful for policy.

Step 10 of 10

Check the model by predicting

The last check uses the model to predict. For out-of-sample prediction, the model estimated on one set of crashes (for example one year) is used to compute outcome probabilities for another set, and the predicted shares are compared with what actually happened. With random parameters the probabilities are again simulated by drawing the parameters many times.
