Basics · in depth

Restricted and unrestricted models, animated

Almost every test in this field compares two versions of the same model: one that is free, and one with some of its numbers locked. This page shows what that means with four animations and five examples.

Part 1 of 7

The idea in plain words

A model is a set of numbers (parameters) that the computer is free to choose so that the model fits the data as well as possible.

Free

Unrestricted model

Every parameter can take whatever value fits best. Think of a made-to-measure suit.

Locked

Restricted model

The same model, but some parameters are locked: forced to be zero, forced to equal each other, or fixed at values borrowed from elsewhere. Think of a suit in one standard size.

The question

The test

Does locking those parameters make the fit much worse, or only a little? Much worse: the locks are wrong. Only a little: the simpler model will do.

Three facts follow, and they are worth memorising.

  • The restricted model is a special case of the unrestricted one. (The two are called nested.)
  • The unrestricted model always fits at least as well, because it could choose the locked values if they were best.
  • So the test never asks “which fits better?” It asks “is the improvement larger than chance alone would produce?”

Animation 3 · What does chance alone look like?

Even when a restriction is exactly true, the unrestricted model fits a little better, because it bends towards the random quirks of the sample. So the statistic is never exactly zero. How big does it get by luck?

The tool below invents studies in which the seat belt truly makes no difference, and computes the statistic for each one. The pile that builds up is the chi-square distribution. The critical value is the point that only 5% of the pile lies beyond. The p-value is the share of the pile beyond your statistic.

Common mistakes

Subtracting the wrong way

The restricted log-likelihood goes first. If your statistic is negative, the two are swapped.

Different data

Both models must be fitted to exactly the same crashes. Dropping a few records for one model breaks the test.

Not nested

A multinomial logit and an ordered probit are not special cases of each other. Use AIC or BIC for those.

Miscounting the locks

Degrees of freedom are the number of parameters locked, not the number of parameters in either model.

“Not rejected” read as “proved”

It only means these data cannot tell the two models apart.

Forgetting which result is the good news

For transferability, rejected is the interesting finding. For a partially constrained model, not rejected is.

Remember it · the locksmith and Judge Chi. A locksmith walks along a row of dials (the parameters) and snaps padlocks onto some of them. Judge Chi watches the needle of a fit-meter drop, doubles the drop, and counts the padlocks to choose his ruler. Few padlocks and a big drop: “Take them off!” Many padlocks and a small drop: “Leave them on. The simple model stands.”
