Research

What current research is doing

Eight recent papers from Analytic Methods in Accident Research, sorted into five themes and explained without jargon. Each theme ends with what is still open, and the page closes with six starter questions for a first research project.

Checked in November 2026 against publisher pages and university repositories. Summaries are based on the published abstracts, not on a full reading; treat them as a map and read the papers before citing them. The Editor-in-Chief’s note (volume 47, 100389) could not be opened, so nothing here is drawn from it.

Time: beyond one model per year

Papers used to stop at “the years differ”. Newer work asks which effects change, for whom, and how to model change without cutting the data into ever smaller pieces.

2024 · article 100335

A systematic unified approach for addressing temporal instability in road safety analysis

Shabab, K. R., Bhowmik, T., Zaki, M. H. and Eluru, N.

Proposes one unified framework for handling temporal instability, in place of estimating a separate model for every period. (Only the title and citation could be checked for this site; read the paper for the method.)

Open the paper

2026 · volume 50, 100428

An exploratory latent segmentation approach to account for temporally shifting parameters in driver injury-severity models

Bhowmik, T., Pervaz, S., Mannering, F. and Eluru, N.

Lets the model sort crashes into hidden segments and asks which segment changes over time. Using three survey years, change was concentrated in a low-injury-risk segment, while the high-risk segment stayed stable.

Open the paper

Still open: Whether effects that look stable over three years stay stable when a fourth year is added; how to forecast when parameters keep drifting.

Groups: let the data suggest the split

Splitting by road class or year is the analyst’s choice. Newer papers let a clustering step or hidden segments propose the groups, then test them with likelihood ratio tests.

2025 · volume 48, 100408

A note on random parameters models of crash injury severities with k-means clustering for data preprocessing

Alnawmasi, N. and Mannering, F.

First groups similar crashes with a simple clustering method, then fits random parameters models with heterogeneity in means and variances to each cluster and year. Likelihood ratio tests supported the clusters, and the clustered models fitted better and gave more precise marginal effects.

Open the paper

Still open: Whether data-driven groups beat the obvious administrative ones (road class, region), and whether they are stable from year to year.

Learning machines against statistical models

Deep learning predicts well. The open question is whether the variable effects it implies can be trusted for deciding countermeasures.

2025 · volume 48, 100405

Analyzing crash injury severities with deep learning and advanced statistical models: An assessment of methodological challenges

Seyfi, M. A., Karimi Mamaghan, A. M., Behnood, A. and Mannering, F.

Compares deep neural networks with random parameters logit models on motorcycle crash severity. The effects of variables implied by deep learning often disagreed with the statistical models in size and sometimes in direction, and shifts over time were hard to handle in deep learning.

Open the paper

Still open: How to get trustworthy variable effects out of a prediction machine, and how to handle shifting effects over time inside one.

Conflicts: how much data, which threshold, which measure

Extreme value theory is now a standard way to turn near misses into crash estimates. Current work is about making it dependable.

2024 · volume 44, 100347

Stochastic method based on copulas for predicting severe road traffic interactions

Chen, Z., Yastremska-Kravchenko, O., Laureshyn, A., Johnsson, C. and D’Agostino, C.

Combines copulas (a way to describe how two measures move together) with extreme value theory, so that interactions of all severities are used, not only the most extreme. Crash-probability estimates were similar to the usual two-measure threshold method, and it handled events the older method could not define.

Open the paper

2024 · volume 44, 100352

A cross-comparison of different extreme value modeling techniques for traffic conflict-based crash risk estimation

Niu, D., Sayed, T., Fu, C. and Mannering, F.

Compares several extreme value approaches for turning conflicts into crash estimates. A threshold that adapts to conditions fitted better than a fixed one, and using two conflict measures together with an adaptive threshold predicted best overall.

Open the paper

2024 · volume 44, 100353

Effects of sample size on pedestrian crash risk estimation from traffic conflicts using extreme value models

Nazir, F., Ali, Y. and Haque, M. M.

Asks how much conflict data is enough. Estimates improved with more data, block-maxima models were more sensitive to sample size, and peak-over-threshold models were more stable. Too small a sample gives unreliable crash estimates with wide uncertainty.

Open the paper

Still open: How much video is enough, how to set the threshold without guesswork, and how to combine several conflict measures.

Good practice: predicting and reporting effects

With random parameters, even computing a prediction or a marginal effect involves choices. Papers that compare those choices give later studies a firmer footing.

2022 · volume 33, 100191

A note on out-of-sample prediction, marginal effects computations, and temporal testing with random parameters crash-injury severity models

Hou, Q., Huo, X., Leng, J. and Mannering, F.

Compared a fixed-parameter multinomial logit with three random-parameter versions. The version with heterogeneity in means and variances predicted best out of sample. Two ways of computing marginal effects both gave defensible results. Testing years pair by pair revealed more detail than one global test.

Open the paper

Still open: A shared standard for reporting marginal effects and out-of-sample checks, so that studies can be compared.

Six starter questions for a first project

These are suggestions for learning the methods on real data. They are not findings, and a literature search should come before any of them.

Idea 1

Add a year

Take a published study design, add the newest year of data, and test whether the effects reported as stable are still stable.

Main tool: Pairwise and global likelihood ratio tests

Idea 2

Cluster first

Compare three ways of splitting the same crashes: by road class, by a clustering step, and by hidden classes. Which gives the best fit and the clearest story?

Main tool: Likelihood ratio tests, AIC and BIC

Idea 3

Machine against model

Fit a random parameters logit and a machine-learning classifier to the same crashes. Compare out-of-sample prediction and, more importantly, whether the two agree on the direction of each variable’s effect.

Main tool: Out-of-sample prediction, marginal effects

Idea 4

Transfer across regions

Estimate a model in one region and test whether it transfers to another. The transferability test is the same one used between road classes.

Main tool: Transferability test

Idea 5

How fragile is the threshold?

With conflict data from one site, estimate crash risk for a range of thresholds and sample sizes and report how much the answer moves.

Main tool: Peak-over-threshold model

Idea 6

Report it twice

Compute marginal effects by two methods for the same model and show where they differ.

Main tool: Average marginal effects

How to read a methods paper in fifteen minutes

  1. Abstract and last paragraph of the introduction (2 min). What is the one methodological claim?
  2. Data section (2 min). What is one observation: a crash, a person, a road segment, a conflict? How many, and over which years?
  3. Method section (4 min). Find the score function. Which of the seven ideas does the model address? Which does it ignore?
  4. Tables (4 min). Find the log-likelihood and the number of parameters. Which parameters are random? Read the marginal effects, not the coefficients.
  5. Tests (2 min). Which likelihood ratio tests were run, and which were rejected?
  6. Conclusions (1 min). Does the paper claim prediction or cause, and did its method earn that claim?
